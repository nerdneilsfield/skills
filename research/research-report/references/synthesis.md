# Synthesis

A report that summarises one source per paragraph has not been synthesised. Organise by concept, and make each paragraph say something no single source said.

## Concept matrix

Keep `matrix.md`: one row per source, one column per concept, dimension, or question. Fill it while writing notes. Webster and Watson (2002) popularised this concept-centric form.

| Source | Problem setting | Approach | Data | Key result | Limits | Trust |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| `smith-2024` | ... | ... | ... | ... | ... | high |

Add columns as concepts emerge, and backfill earlier rows. Read the matrix for what the prose would hide: empty cells are gaps, columns that always agree are consensus, columns that split are the real debates, and sources sharing one dataset or author group are not independent.

## Taxonomy

For a survey, derive the taxonomy from the matrix.

- Split on a difference that changes what a method can do, such as its assumption or its trade-off, not on publication date or on the keyword the authors chose.
- Use one splitting criterion at each level.
- Every included source must have a place. If several do not fit, change the taxonomy instead of forcing them.
- Prefer categories that do not overlap. When a method really spans two, say so.
- Name each category by what it does, so the name teaches.

## Paragraphs

Open with the claim, then give the evidence, explain why it supports the claim, and end by saying what follows from it. Compare explicitly: where methods differ, why, and what each one gives up.

| Weak | Better |
| --- | --- |
| A proposed X. B proposed Y. C proposed Z. | X and Y both assume static scenes; only Z drops this, at the cost of latency [@a; @b; @c]. |
| Many methods have been proposed. | State how many, of which kinds, or cut the sentence. |
| Accuracy improved from 80% to 90%. | Give the dataset, the baseline, and whether the two numbers are comparable. |

## Confidence

Give each key finding a confidence level and its basis:

- **High:** several independent, good sources agree, and the method is sound.
- **Moderate:** the evidence is credible, but limited, indirect, or from one group.
- **Low:** it is sparse, conflicting, or inferred.

Confidence rates the evidence. It does not say how likely the conclusion is, so do not put a confidence level and a probability word in one sentence. Mark your own inference as inference.

For a contested question, list the competing explanations and look for the evidence that separates them, not only the evidence for your favourite.

## Citations

- Take citation metadata from a DOI, a publisher page, or a bibliographic database, never from memory.
- Check that the source says what the sentence claims. When a source only repeats another, trace and cite the original.
- If a citation cannot be verified, write `[CITATION NEEDED]` and report it. Do not substitute a similar paper.

## Review questions

Adapted for this skill from SANRA (Baethge et al., 2019), a quality scale for narrative reviews:

1. Does the opening say why this topic matters to this reader?
2. Are the aims or questions concrete?
3. Is the search described, so a reader can see what was and was not looked at?
4. Are key statements referenced?
5. Is the reasoning shown, including alternatives and limits?
6. Is the data presented with what is needed to judge it, such as sample, baseline, and uncertainty?
7. Could a reader state the main point after reading only the first page?
8. Does each chapter contain something more than the sum of its sources?
