# Report kinds

Pick one kind before collecting sources. The kind decides what the report must prove and how it is organised.

| Kind | Reader's question | Spine | Must contain |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Decision report | What should we do? | The recommendation, then the reasons | Options, criteria, recommendation, what would change it, risks |
| Topic investigation | What is going on here? | Answers to stated questions | Key findings up front, current state, disagreements, what is unknown |
| Survey | How is this field organised, and what is unsolved? | A taxonomy | Scope, selection method, taxonomy, comparison, maturity by area, open problems |
| Systematic review | What does all the evidence say on this narrow question? | The protocol | Protocol fixed first, full search log, screening counts, quality appraisal, synthesis |

If the user asks for a "survey" but has a decision to make, write the decision report and keep the taxonomy as one chapter. Name a systematic review only when the protocol was really followed; a selective read is a narrative survey, and saying so is honest.

## Structures

Explain before you evaluate. Unless `frame.md` says the reader already knows the subject, every kind except a decision memo for experts first says what the subject is and how it works. A reader cannot judge results for a thing nobody has explained.

Use these as starting points. Drop what the material does not support.

**Decision report**

1. Recommendation and the main reasons, on one page
2. Situation and why a decision is needed now
3. Options and decision criteria
4. Comparison against the criteria
5. Risks, and what evidence would change the recommendation
6. Next steps

**Topic investigation**

1. Key findings, each with a confidence level
2. Questions and scope
3. One chapter per finding or question
4. Disagreements and unknowns

**Survey**

1. Introduction: why now, what earlier surveys miss, contributions of this survey
2. Scope and selection: what is in, what is out, how sources were found
3. What the subject is: definition, what it is not, where it came from, with a concrete example
4. How it works: the common process, step by step, with one example traced through
5. Taxonomy, shown as a figure or table
6. One chapter per branch: the steps of its workflow, how it differs, what it trades away
7. Cross-branch comparison, with comparable metrics only
8. Open problems: why each is unsolved, and what a solution would need
9. Conclusion

**Systematic review**

Follow the reporting standard of the field: PRISMA 2020 in general, SEGRESS for software engineering secondary studies. Distinguish records from studies, and give reasons for full-text exclusions.

## Sources

Survey structure: McNabb and Laramee (2019), *How to Write a Visualization Survey Paper*. Answer-first structure: Minto, *The Pyramid Principle*.
